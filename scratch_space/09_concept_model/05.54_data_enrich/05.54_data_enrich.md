# Phase 5.54 - data enrichment: explore first, decide second

Step 4 of [`05.5_cleanup.md`](../05.5_cleanup.md) is under-specified.
It lists fields to promote (examples, lexfile, sense keys, relations) and asserts where they live,
but the placement and the cross-language story are guesses until we look at the data.
This sub-plan fixes that by putting a data-exploration stage in front of the design decisions.

## Principle

Measure before deciding.
Every enrichment choice in Step 4 - what to attach, at what granularity, whether a signal is concept-level or language-level - should be backed by a number from these datasets, not by intuition.
The output of this phase is not new pipeline code; it is a set of findings that turn each open question below into a settled decision, which then flows back into Step 4 and into phases 6 and 7.

The recurring question is **concept-level vs language-level**.
A concept-level fact is a property of the ILI-keyed meaning and is the same in every language (lexfile, hypernym depth, the graph).
A language-level fact belongs to one lemma in one language (token frequency, word length, surface morphology).
The interesting cases are signals we *want* to be cross-language but can only measure in one language (SemCor sense frequency, any CEFR list).
The exploration must test the propagation assumption, not assume it.

## Stage 0 - stage the datasets cleanly, before exploring

Pull and parse every candidate dataset into a tidy, exploration-ready form first, so the topic notebooks read clean inputs instead of re-downloading and re-parsing.
Each staged dataset lands under `data/_raw/lexicon/`, gets a `_build.json` manifest entry (source, version, license), and a thin parsed index.
Nothing here writes the source-of-truth Parquet; this is read-only staging for analysis.

| Dataset | What it gives | Grain | License | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **OMW via `wn`** | examples, lexfile, POS, sense counts, relations | synset / sense (ILI-linked) | permissive, per-lexicon | already present - expose the unused fields to the notebooks |
| **CILI / ILI** | English gloss (fallback, already wired) | ILI record | open | present |
| **Tatoeba** | example sentences | sentence (lemma-join, sense-blind) | CC-BY | download sentences + links, build a per-lemma-per-language index |
| **Wikidata Lexemes** | structured senses, usage examples | lexeme / sense | CC0 | pull a small slice for the five languages, probe coverage only |
| **Token frequency list** | per-language word frequency | lemma surface form | need a permissive one (e.g. wordfreq) | stage one list per language, record its source corpus and license |
| **Graded vocabulary / CEFR** | difficulty labels for validation | word / lemma | need a permissive list | find one (English first); used only to validate complexity signals, not shipped |

Open Stage-0 questions to resolve while staging:

- Are OMW `examples` / `lexfile` / relations attached to the ILI / English synset only, or also to the per-language lexicons (pt/es/fr/it)?
  This decides whether they are concept-level (propagate for free) or need per-language joins.
- Is there a permissive token-frequency source for all five languages from one consistent corpus, or do we mix corpora (and lose comparability)?
- Does a permissive CEFR / graded list exist for at least English so the complexity signals can be validated against ground truth?

## Topic 1 - examples

What we want: example sentences attached at the most precise granularity each source actually supports, never inventing a sense link a source does not provide.

Candidate datasets: OMW `synset.examples()` (concept-level, permissive), Tatoeba (lemma-level, sense-blind, CC-BY), Wikidata usage examples (lexeme-level, CC0, likely sparse).

Open questions:

- Coverage: what fraction of concepts have at least one OMW example, per language and overall? distribution of examples per synset?
- Are OMW examples English-only, or do non-en lexicons carry their own?
- Tatoeba: how many sentences per lemma per language? what share of those lemmas are polysemous (so a lemma-join is genuinely sense-blind and must be flagged)?
- How much would Tatoeba actually add on top of OMW examples, and is the CC-BY attribution cost worth that delta?

Exploration that answers them:

- Count synsets with `>= 1` example, split by language; histogram of examples-per-synset.
- Cross-tabulate Tatoeba sentence counts against our lemmas; for the matched lemmas, measure the polysemy rate (senses-per-lemma) to quantify how sense-blind the join is.
- Estimate the incremental coverage: concepts with no OMW example that Tatoeba could cover.

Decision this unlocks: whether examples ship from OMW only in this phase (permissive, concept-level, fan out to senses) and Tatoeba stays deferred, or Tatoeba is worth wiring now with a sense-blind flag.

## Topic 2 - categories / POS

What we want: a coarse semantic class per concept (lexfile, e.g. `noun.motion`) for clustering and difficulty, and a clean POS.

Candidate datasets: OMW `synset.lexfile`, POS from synset and lemma.

Open questions:

- lexfile coverage: what fraction of synsets carry one, and how many distinct lexfiles exist?
- Is lexfile concept-level (a property of the Princeton synset / ILI, so identical across languages) or only present on the English synset?
- POS distribution; how many rows carry the odd / unmapped POS values (`s`, `p`, `x`, `u`) flagged in the 05.4 checks, and do they map cleanly to our POS set?

Exploration that answers them:

- Count synsets by lexfile; report the distinct set and the long tail.
- Check lexfile presence on non-en synsets to confirm whether it propagates from the ILI or must be looked up on the English synset.
- POS histogram across all five languages; isolate the odd-POS rows for the Step 5 review.

Decision this unlocks: lexfile becomes a concept-level field promoted in Step 4 (feeding 6/8 clustering), with a confirmed propagation rule; POS cleanup scope is sized.

## Topic 3 - SemCor and cross-language frequency propagation

This is the topic with the real analysis. There are two distinct frequency signals and they must not be conflated:

- **Concept commonness** - how common a *meaning* is. The hypothesis from the brief: if a concept is common, it is common in every language. This is concept-level and should propagate across languages.
- **Lemma token frequency** - how common a *word form* is in one language. This is language-level and comes from a per-language frequency list.

SemCor sits between them. `sense.counts()` / `tag_count` is the WordNet sense-tagged frequency, English only, per sense.
It is the only direct per-sense frequency we have, and phase 6 wants it as the weights that split a lemma's token frequency across its senses.
The problem: we have those weights for English only.

Open questions:

- SemCor coverage: what fraction of English senses have `tag_count > 0`? distribution of the counts (how skewed)?
- Do any non-en lexicons carry sense counts at all (expected: no)?
- If we aggregate SemCor sense counts up to the ILI / concept level, does the resulting "concept commonness" ranking look sensible, and does it correlate with an independent English lemma-frequency list?
- Can the English sense-distribution serve as a prior for splitting another language's lemma token frequency across that lemma's senses, given the senses share the same ILI? How wrong is that prior on a handful of hand-checked polysemous words?

Exploration that answers them:

- Fraction of en senses with a count; count distribution (log scale); top and bottom examples.
- Build a prototype concept-commonness score: sum SemCor counts over the senses of each ILI, rank concepts, eyeball the head and tail for plausibility.
- Correlate that concept score against a per-language token-frequency list (English first, then es/it) by mapping concept -> its lemmas in that language -> their frequencies. A positive correlation supports the "common concept = common word everywhere" hypothesis.
- For a small set of polysemous lemmas in a non-English language, compare the English SemCor sense split against any available evidence to gauge how usable it is as a prior.

Propagation design the findings drive:

- If concept commonness propagates (correlation holds), carry a concept-level commonness signal derived from SemCor and use it cross-language; per-language ranking then refines it with that language's token frequency.
- If the English sense split transfers acceptably, use it as the phase-6 prior for languages without sense-tagged corpora; if not, fall back to lemma-level frequency with no sense split for those languages.
- This phase only confirms the signals exist and carries `sense.id` through the build so phase 6 can join them; the weighting math is phase 6.

## Topic 4 - relations

What we want: the relation graph the `ConceptRelation` / `FalseFriendRelation` stubs already expect - hypernym / hyponym at minimum, antonym, and whatever else is cheap.

Candidate datasets: OMW relations - synset-level (hypernym / hyponym / holonym / meronym, similar-to) and sense-level (antonym, derivation). All keyed on ILI, so concept-level.

Open questions:

- Which relation types does `wn` actually expose for our lexicons, and at what coverage each?
- Are relations on the English / ILI synset only, or replicated per language? (If ILI-keyed, they are concept-level and resolve onto our ILI-grouped concepts directly.)
- Edge counts and degree distribution; how many concepts are isolated (degree 0), which matters because connectivity is a ranking signal in 05.4 / phase 6?

Exploration that answers them:

- Count edges by relation type; report coverage per type.
- Degree distribution over concepts; count isolated concepts.
- Verify a sample of edges resolves cleanly to ILI-grouped concept ids in our current build (no dangling edges).

Decision this unlocks: which relation types Step 4 captures in the OMW traversal (feeding phase 7), and a connectivity metric available to the phase-6 ranking.

## Topic 5 - complexity

What we want: a difficulty signal per concept, and where needed per lemma, ideally mostly cross-language.

Hypothesis from the brief: an obscure concept is a hard, unusual word in every language, so complexity is largely a concept-level property with a thin per-language lexical overlay.
If true, we compute most of complexity once at the concept level and adjust per language.

Candidate inputs, all derived from the topics above:

- Concept-level: concept commonness (Topic 3), hypernym depth in the tree (Topic 4, depth as a proxy for specificity), lexfile / abstractness (Topic 2).
- Language-level overlay: lemma token frequency rank, word length, simple morphology.

Open questions:

- Which inputs actually predict difficulty, rather than just feeling like they should?
- Is the concept-level part enough, or is the per-language overlay doing real work?
- Is there a permissive CEFR / graded list to validate against, for at least one language?

Exploration that answers them:

- Assemble the candidate signals per concept and per lemma for one or two languages.
- Correlate each signal against a held-out CEFR / graded list (English first): which carry signal, which are redundant.
- Test the cross-language hypothesis directly: take concepts that are "easy" in English (high commonness, low depth) and check whether their es / it lemmas are also high-frequency; quantify how often the concept-level call disagrees with the per-language reality.

Decision this unlocks: the shape of the phase-6 complexity score - how much is concept-level (computed once, propagated) versus a per-language overlay - grounded in which signals validated.

## Exploration deliverables

A small set of numbered exploration notebooks under `notebooks/lexicon_ingest/` (or a dedicated `notebooks/lexicon_enrich/`), one per topic plus a Stage-0 staging notebook.
Each notebook is a thin caller over staged inputs, logs with loguru, and ends with a short findings cell stating the numbers and the decision they imply.
The findings get folded back into Step 4 of 05.5 and into phases 6 / 7; this folder can hold per-topic findings notes if a topic needs more than a notebook cell.
