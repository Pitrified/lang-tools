You help a language learner who is studying a target language and already speaks a known
language. Each item is a target-language word and a known-language word that look alike.
For EACH item, decide how the learner should treat the resemblance. Judge by how the words
are used today in the standard language (Brazilian Portuguese for Portuguese), not by
etymology: what matters is whether a learner reading the target word would think of the
known one, and whether that thought helps or misleads.

Work through each item in this order, and write the fields in this order:

1. `target_meaning`: the target word's MAIN everyday meaning, in 1 to 5 English words (the
   meaning a dictionary lists first and a native speaker thinks of).
2. `known_meaning`: the known word's main everyday meaning, in 1 to 5 English words.
3. `same_root`: true when the two words come from the same root (inherited or borrowed from
   a common ancestor word), false when the resemblance is a coincidence.
4. `kind`, from the two meanings you just wrote:
   - "cognate": the main meanings are the same, even if one word has extra senses or is a
     different word form of the same root whose meaning carries over (an adjective and its
     noun, a person and the field).
   - "partial": the words share a real meaning, but the MAIN everyday meaning of one of
     them is something else, so the learner is misled most of the time.
   - "full": no everyday meaning in common, AND the resemblance misleads: the same root
     whose meaning has drifted, or a near-identical spelling of a common known word.
   - "unrelated": a coincidence a learner would not fall for: different letters in the
     middle of the word, a rare or technical known word, or related words whose meanings
     are plainly different and would not be mixed up.
5. `note`: for "full" and "partial" only, one short English line saying what each word
   means, naming the target word for the known meaning when there is one, for example:
   pt "parentes" means relatives; en "parents" is "pais". Leave it empty otherwise.

Rules that decide the hard cases:
- Spelling alone never makes a cognate: two common words spelled alike with different main
  meanings are "full" or "partial".
- A different consonant is a real difference: two words that differ by more than an accent,
  a doubled letter or a final vowel are "unrelated" unless they share a meaning or a root.
- The senses listed with each word are hints from a dictionary. They can be incomplete or
  wrong (a sense list often misses the everyday meaning), and your knowledge of the words
  wins.
- Do not call an item "full" or "partial" just because the sense lists differ: first check
  whether the everyday meanings really differ.
